Forecasts · CDC FluSight 2026–27

Weekly influenza hospital admissions

The CDC’s FluSight competition collects the best influenza forecasts possible from contributing teams across the United States, so they can be used for hospital preparedness (e.g., staffing, supplies, bed capacity) and for producing and distributing vaccines (e.g., timing pharmacy advertisements for the flu shot).

My team (NAU_PyBNF) submits a four-week forecast every week: the current week plus three weeks ahead.

These forecasts offer real-time insight into the current state of influenza transmission across the nation and in individual U.S. states and territories, so that larger institutions (e.g., the CDC, hospitals) and individuals can make informed decisions about travel, vaccination, supplies, staffing, and more.

    Forecasts like the ones submitted to FluSight are probabilistic. Instead of predicting a single value for influenza hospitalizations over the next few weeks, we submit a range of values based on the noise in the data and the error in our models (a prediction interval). The first thing to look at is the median forecast, the solid line: what we believe is most likely over the next few weeks. The shaded bands around it are our prediction intervals: admissions could go that high or that low, depending on how transmission plays out over the next few weeks.

    The models

    Oracle SIHRS

    NAU_PyBNF-OracleSIHRS · mechanistic

    The SIHRS model my team submits every week assumes that flu is transmitted directly from person to person, that individual immunity to flu strains (e.g., H3N2, H1N1) wanes over time, and that some people can be reinfected in future epidemics.

    The model is fitted with a Liu–West particle filter: an algorithm that parameterizes the model from real-time data, carrying about 10,000 possible epidemic scenarios and weighting each one by how closely it matches the observed data.

    The Oracle step then takes growth ratios from the GroundHogCGR model’s bank of past seasons and blends them, half and half, into each of the 10,000 particle trajectories after each new week of real-time data. This gives the mechanistic model, which is fitted only to this season’s data, a “sense” of what growth may look like based on past seasons.

    Groundhog

    NAU_PyBNF-GroundHogCGR · empirical

    The Groundhog Calendar Growth Ratio model (GroundHogCGR) is a purely empirical model that we compare our mechanistic models against: it uses only historical data, and the patterns within that data, to predict the future.

    Specifically, it takes donor growth (the growth of influenza hospitalizations) from past seasons within a five-week window of the current date, and assumes that the new growth of an epidemic can be explained by similar calendar-pinned growth in past seasons.
